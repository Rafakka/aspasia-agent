Aspasia

470 BCE – 400 BCE · rhetorician · philosopher · writer

6 min read · 8 sources

Kept by The Keeper · written from cited sources · AI-assisted draft, not yet human-reviewed · How records are made

Aspasia of Miletus was a rhetorician, philosopher, and writer who became the partner of the Athenian statesman Pericles and one of the most discussed women of fifth-century BC Greece. Born around 470 BCE in Miletus, she lived at the center of Athenian intellectual life during the city's golden age, and ancient writers credited her with teaching rhetoric and conversing with Socrates. Because comedy, philosophy, and courtroom gossip all shaped her record, any biography of Aspasia must be built from fragments, yet those fragments describe a woman of unusual learning and influence. She died in Athens around 400 BCE, leaving a legacy that scholars still debate.

Early Life

Aspasia was born around 470 BCE in Miletus, a prosperous Greek city on the Ionian coast of Asia Minor, in what is now western Turkey [1]. Ancient sources name her father as Axiochus, which suggests a family of some standing, since the name recurs among well-connected Greeks of the period [2]. Miletus had a long reputation for learning: it produced Thales, Anaximander, and Anaximenes, the earliest known Greek natural philosophers, and its schools and trade links exposed residents to ideas from across the eastern Mediterranean.

Nothing certain survives about her childhood or education, a gap typical for Greek women of the era, whose lives went largely unrecorded unless they touched public affairs. Scholars infer from her later reputation for eloquence that she received an education far beyond what Athenian citizen women were normally offered [1]. At some point, probably in the 450s BCE, she moved to Athens. As a Milesian she counted as a resident foreigner there, a metic, a legal status that barred her from marriage to an Athenian citizen but also freed her from some of the seclusion expected of citizen wives [3].

Path to Prominence

Athens in the mid fifth century BCE was the wealthiest and most ambitious city in the Greek world, and its leading figure was Pericles, the general and statesman who dominated its democracy for decades. Aspasia entered his circle by the mid 440s BCE, after Pericles had divorced or separated from his first wife, and the two formed a lasting partnership [4]. Because Pericles himself had sponsored a law in 451 BCE restricting Athenian citizenship to children of two citizen parents, he could not legally marry a Milesian, so Aspasia lived with him as his acknowledged companion rather than his wife [3].

Her household became a gathering place for the thinkers who gave the age its character. Plutarch, writing centuries later but drawing on classical sources, reports that Socrates visited her with his associates and that some men brought their wives to hear her speak, remarkable behavior in a city where respectable women rarely appeared in mixed company [2]. Plato's dialogue Menexenus goes further, presenting Socrates reciting a funeral oration he claims Aspasia composed, and jokingly calling her his instructor in rhetoric [5]. However playful Plato's intent, the passage shows that fourth-century readers found the idea of Aspasia as a teacher of speechmaking plausible enough to build a dialogue around.

For anyone asking who Aspasia was in the eyes of her contemporaries, the answer depends on the witness. Comic playwrights mocked her, philosophers wrote her into dialogues, and later biographers repeated both traditions. Modern historians treat each strand cautiously, since Athenian comedy routinely slandered public figures and Socratic literature freely invented conversations [1].

Major Achievements

Aspasia's achievements survive mainly through the testimony of others, since none of her own writings remain. The most consistent claim across ancient sources is her mastery of rhetoric, the art of persuasive speech that stood at the heart of Athenian public life. Besides Plato's Menexenus, the Socratic writers Aeschines of Sphettus and Antisthenes each composed a dialogue titled Aspasia, evidence that she was a recognized intellectual subject within a generation of her death [5]. Aeschines' dialogue, known from fragments and later summaries, portrayed her advising a couple on marriage through skillful questioning, a technique resembling the Socratic method itself [1].

Cicero, writing in Rome in the first century BCE, preserved part of that dialogue in his treatise De Inventione and treated Aspasia's questioning as a model of argument by induction [6]. That a Roman handbook on rhetoric cited a Greek woman as an exemplar, some 350 years after her lifetime, says a great deal about the durability of her reputation. Historians of rhetoric now count her among the earliest women whose contribution to the discipline can be documented at all, even if only at second hand [7].

Ancient writers also credited her with political influence. Plutarch records the tradition that Pericles consulted her on public matters and that some Athenians blamed her for the Samian War of 440 BCE, fought against a rival of Miletus, though he presents the charge as gossip rather than established fact [2]. Among the facts about Aspasia that can be stated with confidence, the strongest is simply this: no other woman of classical Athens generated so much writing from the philosophers, comedians, and historians of her own era.

Personal Life

Aspasia and Pericles lived together for roughly a decade and a half, and ancient anecdote presents the bond as affectionate by the standards of the time. Plutarch relates a story that Pericles kissed her each day when leaving for and returning from the agora, a detail his sources found worth remarking on precisely because public displays of marital warmth were unusual in Athens [2]. Around 440 BCE the couple had a son, Pericles the Younger, who under his father's own citizenship law was excluded from Athenian citizenship [3].

Her position also drew hostility. Comic poets of the day, including Cratinus and Aristophanes, attacked her on stage; Aristophanes' play Acharnians of 425 BCE even blamed the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War on a farcical quarrel involving Aspasia, a joke that traded on her notoriety [4]. A later tradition, reported by Plutarch, holds that the comic poet Hermippus prosecuted her for impiety and that Pericles wept while pleading for her acquittal, though many modern scholars suspect the trial story grew out of a comedy rather than a real courtroom [1].

The family suffered heavily during the plague that struck Athens after 430 BCE. Pericles lost his two legitimate sons to the epidemic, and the assembly then granted citizenship to Pericles the Younger as an exception to the law, legitimizing Aspasia's child shortly before Pericles himself died of the plague in 429 BCE [4].

Later Years

After Pericles' death, Aspasia formed a union with Lysicles, an Athenian trader in livestock who rose to a position of political leadership. The connection struck ancient observers as evidence of her influence: Aeschines of Sphettus claimed that her guidance turned Lysicles from a sheep dealer into a prominent public speaker [5]. The partnership was brief, since Lysicles died on campaign in 428 BCE, only a year or so after Pericles [1].

The historical record then falls almost silent. She apparently remained in Athens raising her son, who grew up to serve the city as a general. In 406 BCE, Pericles the Younger commanded ships at the naval victory of Arginusae, then was condemned and executed along with fellow generals for failing to rescue shipwrecked crews, one of the most notorious judicial episodes of the late war years [3]. Whether Aspasia lived to see her son's death is unknown. Most modern accounts place her own death in Athens around 400 BCE, a date reasoned from the chronology of the Socratic writers who knew of her, rather than from any surviving notice of her passing [1].

Legacy

Few figures from classical Greece have been reinvented as often as Aspasia. To the comic stage she was a schemer; to the Socratics, a teacher of eloquence; to Renaissance and Enlightenment painters, an emblem of learned conversation. Nineteenth-century European artists and novelists made her a romantic heroine of the Athenian golden age, and the painter Jean-Léon Gérôme placed her at the center of his 1861 canvas Socrates Seeking Alcibiades in the House of Aspasia [8].

Modern scholarship has worked to separate the woman from the caricatures. Madeleine Henry's 1995 study traced how each era projected its own anxieties about intelligent women onto her, from Athenian comedy to modern fiction, and argued that the sources reveal more about her interpreters than about Aspasia herself [1]. Historians of rhetoric, meanwhile, have restored her to surveys of the discipline's origins, citing the Socratic and Ciceronian testimony as the earliest usable evidence for a woman teaching persuasive speech in the Greek world [7].

Her story also illuminates the limits placed on even exceptional women in classical Athens. She could shape conversation in the most powerful household in Greece, yet she could not marry, vote, or speak in the assembly, and her son needed a special decree to become a citizen [3]. That tension between documented ability and legal exclusion keeps Aspasia's achievements at the center of debates about gender in the ancient world, and it explains why a woman who left no writings of her own still commands a place in every serious history of the age of Pericles.

Questions & Answers

When was Aspasia born?
Aspasia was born around 470 BCE in Miletus, a Greek city on the Ionian coast of Asia Minor in present-day Turkey. The exact year is an estimate, since no birth records survive for Greek women of her era.
What is Aspasia famous for?
Aspasia is famous as the partner of the Athenian statesman Pericles and as a skilled rhetorician in fifth-century BC Athens. Ancient writers, including Plato in his dialogue Menexenus, portrayed her as a teacher of persuasive speech who conversed with Socrates. She is one of the earliest women whose role in the history of rhetoric can be documented.
Was Aspasia married to Pericles?
No, Aspasia and Pericles never legally married, because an Athenian law of 451 BCE, which Pericles himself had sponsored, barred marriage between citizens and foreigners. As a woman from Miletus she was a resident alien in Athens, so the couple lived together as acknowledged partners. Their son, Pericles the Younger, was later granted citizenship by special decree.
Did Aspasia know Socrates?
Several ancient sources connect Aspasia with Socrates and his circle. Plutarch reports that Socrates visited her household with his companions, and Plato's Menexenus has Socrates jokingly call her his teacher of rhetoric. Two other followers of Socrates, Aeschines of Sphettus and Antisthenes, each wrote a dialogue named after her.
How did Aspasia die?
No ancient source records the cause or exact circumstances of Aspasia's death. Modern historians estimate that she died in Athens around 400 BCE, a date inferred from the chronology of the Socratic writers who mention her.
Did Aspasia have children?
Aspasia had one known son, Pericles the Younger, born around 440 BCE to her and Pericles. He was granted Athenian citizenship by special decree after his father's legitimate sons died in the plague. He later served as a general and was executed in 406 BCE after the battle of Arginusae.
